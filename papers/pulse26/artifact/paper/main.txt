4    Key Points
5      • The 26 s Gulf of Guinea microseism is tidally modulated: its power rises by about 11% (0.47
6        dB) 1.6 h after M2 high water

7      • Five stations 240–3670 km from the source share one tidal phase, about 1.8 h after high
8        water, so the modulation arises at the source

9      • Linear tidal water-depth eﬀects at shelf depths are 4–40 times too weak; very shallow coupling
10       or a nonlinear source is indicated


11   Plain Language Summary
12   For more than sixty years seismometers around the world have recorded a faint, almost pure
13   seismic “hum” with a period of about 26 seconds coming from the Gulf of Guinea, oﬀ West Africa.
14   Nobody knows for sure what produces it. We looked at seven years of recordings to see whether
15   the hum responds to the ocean tide. It does: it is about 11% stronger in power roughly one and
16   a half to two hours after high tide than near low tide. Stations in Cameroon, Algeria, Uganda,
17   St Helena and Cape Verde all see the same timing, so the eﬀect happens where the hum is made,
18   not near the seismometers. The rise and fall of the sea surface over the continental shelf is far
19   too small to explain such a large change through the simple wave physics we tested. The source
20   is therefore either in very shallow water near the coast or it responds to the tide in a strongly
21   nonlinear way. The tidal response gives a new, quantitative test for any proposed explanation of
22   this long-standing puzzle.

23                                                  Abstract

24        A narrow 26 s seismic line radiated from the Gulf of Guinea has been observed since the 1960s,
25        but its source remains unexplained. Using 7.4 years of continuous data at station G.EDA
26        (Cameroon) and six more distant stations, we show that the line is modulated by the ocean
27        tide. At EDA, line power corrected for background noise varies with an M2 amplitude of 0.47
28        dB (95% conﬁdence interval 0.42–0.53 dB, about ±11% in power), peaking 1.6 h after high
29        water at Limbe. The modulation survives conservative block-resampled null tests (p ≤ 0.001)
30        and appears with a common lag (1.8 h; 1.5–2.1 h) at ﬁve stations 240–3670 km away, so it
31        originates at the source. Its amplitude grows with tidal range, consistent with, but not proof
32        of, proportional scaling. Linear tidal changes in water depth at shelf depths are 4–40 times too
33        weak, which points to very shallow coupling or a nonlinear source.

34   1     Introduction
35   A persistent, nearly monochromatic seismic line near 26 s period (0.038 Hz) is radiated from the
36   Gulf of Guinea. Long-period microseisms of 26–27 s were ﬁrst reported as a worldwide “storm”
37   by Oliver (1962); Holcomb (1980, 1998) showed that a 26 s spectral line is a recurrent feature of
38   the long-period background, and Shapiro et al. (2006) located its source in the Bight of Bonny
39   using ambient-noise cross-correlations. Xia et al. (2013) identiﬁed two distinct persistent sources,
40   near 0.038 Hz and 0.036 Hz, and a related 16 s persistent tremor has since been described (Xia
41   and Chen, 2020; Takano and Poli, 2025). The source of the 26 s line has also been associated with
42   gliding tremors (Bruland and Hadziioannou, 2023).
43        The generation mechanism is debated. Early work linked 26 s microseisms to long-period swell
44   and primary-microseism coupling in shallow water (Bernard and Martel, 1990), the standard mech-
45   anism for primary microseisms (Hasselmann, 1963; Ardhuin, 2018), while the classical secondary
46   mechanism operates at half the wave period (Longuet-Higgins, 1950). Others have argued for a
47   solid-Earth source: magnitude–frequency and inter-event statistics consistent with a crack network
48   driven by random processes (Chen et al., 2022), or vibration of thin crustal plates above magmatic
49   sills (Xia et al., 2024). Most recently, Poli et al. (2026) showed that bursts of 26 s and 16 s energy
50   follow the arrival of long-period swell from Southern Ocean storms, and proposed that swells excite
51   a resonant structure.
52        Tidal modulation is a classical probe of how a seismic source responds to small, known stresses
53   (e.g., Rubinstein et al., 2008), but to our knowledge it has not been tested for the 26 s line. In the
54   peer-review ﬁle of Poli et al. (2026) the authors argued that the small tidal changes in water depth
55   should not matter for the propagation of very long swells in tens of metres of water, while noting
56   that tides could modulate surf-zone properties. Here we test for tidal modulation directly, using
57   multi-year continuous records at a near-source station and six distant stations, and we compare
58   its size and timing with simple physical expectations.


59   2     Data and Methods
60   2.1   Seismic data and line metric
61   We use vertical long-period (LHZ, 1 sample/s) records from G.EDA (Cameroon; 240 km from a
62   nominal source point at 4◦ N, 8◦ E) for 2019-05-04 to 2026-09-28, and G.TAM (Algeria; 2096 km)
63   for 2022-03 to 2026-09. For a common period, 2022-03 to 2024-02, we add II.MBAR (Uganda;
64   2580 km), II.SHEL (St Helena; 2677 km), II.ASCN (Ascension; 2812 km), GE.WIN (Namibia;
65   3101 km) and II.SACV (Cape Verde; 3670 km) (Figure 1). Instrument responses were removed
66   with ObsPy (Beyreuther et al., 2010).
67       Spectra were computed in 3-h windows with a 1-h step (Hann-tapered periodograms, and Welch
68   spectra from 1-h segments). Windows contaminated by earthquakes or glitches were rejected when
69   20–30 mHz power exceeded ﬁve times its 15-day running median or when the peak-to-median
70   absolute amplitude ratio exceeded 30; at EDA 38,888 of 52,547 windows (74.0%) pass. The line
71   is “detected” when its Welch signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) exceeds the 99th percentile of the same
72   statistic in a line-free control band (30–32.5 mHz), i.e. at a 1% false-alarm rate. Our tidal metric
73   does not depend on detection: it is the mean power in a ﬁxed band on the line (37.5–38.6 mHz,
74   in dB), minus a linear regression on the power in two background bands (20–24 s and 29–34
75   s), computed for every quality-controlled hour (“line minus background”, LMB). The regression


                                                       2
76    removes site, weather and propagation noise common to the line and its spectral neighbourhood
77    (EDA coeﬃcients 0.24 and 0.49). Because the 29–34 s band at the strongest stations is itself
78    weakly modulated near the line phase (full span: 0.21 dB at +0.2 h at EDA, 0.17 dB at +1.1 h
79    at TAM), the LMB amplitude is a conservative estimate (raw line power: 0.548 dB at EDA, 0.473
80    dB at TAM).

81    2.2   Tides
82    The tide reference is the UHSLC research-quality hourly record at Port Sonara (Limbe, Cameroon;
83    4.01◦ N, 9.13◦ E) for 2008-06 to 2011-06 (Caldwell et al., 2001). A harmonic ﬁt with UTide (Codiga,
84    2011; Pawlowicz et al., 2002) explains 98.9% of the variance (M2 amplitude 0.59 m, S2 0.21 m)
85    and was used to predict the tide for 2019–2026; no contemporaneous gauge record is available.
86    Fits at São Tomé and Lagos give M2 high water within −0.18 and +0.29 h of Port Sonara. Lags
87    below are hours after predicted M2 high water at Port Sonara.

88    2.3   Statistics
89    Each hourly series was high-passed with a 72-h running median and regressed jointly on M2,
90    S2, N2, K1, O1, Q1, M4, S1 and S3; the modulation amplitude is the modulus of the complex
91    coeﬃcient (dB, half the peak-to-trough range) and the lag is the time of maximum. Conﬁdence
92    intervals come from a moving-block bootstrap with 30-day blocks (Künsch, 1989) (15-day blocks
93    give the same results). Signiﬁcance was assessed against three nulls of increasing conservatism:
94    phase-randomised surrogates (Theiler et al., 1992) and, most conservatively, random-start block
95    resampling, in which 15- or 30-day blocks of data are moved to random tidal phases while the design
96    matrix is ﬁxed (1000 realisations each). Multi-station phases were combined by inverse-variance
97    weighting with a joint (same-block) bootstrap that preserves inter-station correlation.
98        To test how the response scales with tidal range we (i) ﬁtted the in-phase M2-band response
99    within ﬁve quantile bins of the semidiurnal tidal envelope E(t), and (ii) ﬁtted the envelope model
100   y = Re[(P + Qe) eiθ ], with e = E/Ē − 1 and tidal phase θ, so that γ = Re(Q/P ) is 0 for constant
101   amplitude and 1 for amplitude proportional to tidal range. Constituent admittances (e.g. the
102   S2/M2 ratio of the seismic response divided by that of the gauge) provide an independent version
103   of the same test.

104   2.4   Toy model
105   To gauge the expected size of linear depth eﬀects we computed the M2 modulation of several
106   quantities for 26 s swell under an M2 tide of 0.59 m: bottom pressure and bed orbital velocity
107   (Airy waves with shoaling), primary-microseism forcing with a wavy-bottom spectrum FB ∝ k −2 or
108   k −3 (Hasselmann, 1963; Ardhuin, 2018), depth-limited (breaking-type) forcing, and wave–current
109   interaction for a standing tide.


110   3     Results
111   3.1   A tidal line in the 26 s power
112   The line is detected in 31.0% of quality-controlled hours at EDA (median period 26.341 s, 95%
113   CI 26.336–26.347 s; Figure 1c) and in 14.1% at TAM. In the LMB periodogram the M2 frequency
114   stands out at both stations, with peak-to-median power ratios of 147 (EDA) and 130 (TAM) against

                                                      3
115   95% surrogate band maxima of 8.7 and 6.6 (Figure 2a,b). At EDA the M2 amplitude is 0.473 dB
116   (95% CI 0.422–0.529 dB) with a lag of +1.63 h (1.46–1.82 h). None of the 1000 block-resampled
117   surrogates reaches this amplitude for either block length (p ≤ 0.001; z = 17.3 and 13.5 for 15- and
118   30-day blocks), nor do any of 500 phase-randomised surrogates. The bootstrap variance-inﬂation
119   factor is 2.24, i.e. an eﬀective sample of 17,336 hours. TAM gives 0.398 dB (0.346–0.455 dB) at
120   +1.66 h (1.45–1.89 h). Power binned by tidal phase shows a smooth, nearly sinusoidal cycle at
121   EDA, TAM and SACV (Figure 2c).
122       The result does not depend on the metric: the raw ﬁxed-band line power (0.548 dB, +1.3 h),
123   the Welch SNR (0.391 dB, +1.7 h) and the detection fraction, which rises from about 26% near
124   low water to 36% near high water (M2 amplitude 5.5 percentage points), all show it. S2 (0.168
125   dB, +2.2 h) and N2 (0.075 dB, +2.4 h) are also signiﬁcant under block resampling; M4 is small
126   (0.033 dB). Dropping any one calendar year changes the EDA amplitude to 0.448–0.492 dB and
127   the lag to 1.57–1.67 h. Single-year amplitudes range from 0.284 to 0.602 dB and are lowest in
128   2024–2026, while single-year lags stay within 1.33–1.85 h. Adding ERA5 signiﬁcant wave height
129   and peak period (Hersbach et al., 2020) as covariates leaves the M2 term unchanged (0.473 dB).

130   3.2   Same phase at ﬁve stations: a source eﬀect
131   Over the common period the M2 term passes all three nulls at EDA, TAM, MBAR, SHEL and
132   SACV (Figure 3). The lags are +1.75 h (95% CI 1.31–2.14 h) at EDA, +1.75 h (1.47–2.10 h)
133   at TAM, +2.40 h (1.00–3.57 h) at MBAR, +1.99 h (0.96–2.82 h) at SHEL and +1.82 h (1.25–
134   2.41 h) at SACV, with amplitudes of 0.513, 0.445, 0.109, 0.159 and 0.274 dB. MBAR is marginal
135   (p ≈ 0.03 under block resampling). The ﬁve lags are statistically homogeneous (p = 0.88) with a
136   weighted mean of +1.80 h (1.49–2.10 h). These stations span 240–3670 km and azimuths from 96◦
137   to 353◦ , and their local tides, oceanic settings and noise diﬀer. The 20–24 s background bands, in
138   contrast, peak 6.0–8.0 h after high water at all seven stations, and the 29–34 s bands peak 6–10
139   h after high water at MBAR, SHEL, ASCN and SACV (Figure 3). A common phase of the line
140   at these distances, absent in the background, places the modulation at the source rather than at
141   the receivers or along the paths. II.ASCN and GE.WIN detect the line in only about 2% and
142   3% of hours and show no signiﬁcant M2 term (p ≈ 0.3–0.4): they are uninformative rather than
143   contradictory.

144   3.3   Scaling with tidal range
145   The response grows from neap to spring tides (Figure 4a). Across ﬁve envelope bins (mean envelope
146   0.34–0.84 m) the EDA in-phase amplitude rises from 0.35 to 0.66 dB; a constant amplitude is
147   rejected (p = 4 × 10−4 ; TAM p = 7 × 10−4 ) and proportional scaling is acceptable (p = 0.32;
148   TAM 0.28). The envelope model gives γ = 0.70 (0.41–0.99) at EDA and 0.68 (0.35–1.05) at TAM
149   over the full spans, and 0.96 (0.56–1.37) at EDA for the spring–neap (M2+S2) envelope alone.
150   Consistently, the S2 admittance relative to M2 is 0.99 (0.70–1.30) at EDA, i.e. the S2/M2 ratio of
151   the seismic response (0.355) matches that of the tide (0.360). In short, γ ≈ 0.7–1, and proportional
152   scaling is not excluded.
153       We do not regard the shortfall below 1 as robust. It is metric-dependent (raw line power gives
154   γ = 1.01 at EDA but 0.32 at TAM), it comes mostly from the weak perigean (N2) leg of the
155   envelope, and plausible errors in the predicted envelope bias γ by only about 6–12% (Supporting
156   Information). A purely lunar test is weak because the lunar-only envelope varies by only about
157   ±20%: with a complete lunar envelope it gives γ = 0.88 (0.07–1.72) at TAM, consistent with

                                                       4
158   proportionality, and 0.45 (−0.20 to 1.16) at EDA, inconclusive. In 2-yr windows γ is poorly
159   constrained (standard deviation 0.27–0.32 at EDA and TAM, 0.44–0.50 at SACV). The 2022–2024
160   values are low at all three stations (SACV −0.26, −0.88 to 0.52), partly because the stations share
161   noise; we cannot distinguish this from a modest real change in the source’s tidal sensitivity.

162   3.4   Spectral structure
163   The 28 s line is also modulated, more weakly and slightly later (0.16 dB at +2.5 h at EDA and
164   0.17 dB at +2.3 h at TAM, after local-background removal). Both components of the 26 s doublet
165   are modulated by the same amount (EDA 0.61 and 0.63 dB in raw power). We ﬁnd no robust
166   tidal modulation of the line frequency. An apparent M2 frequency term (14 µHz at EDA, 38 µHz
167   at TAM) peaks in antiphase with the line SNR, is larger at the lower-SNR station, and is not
168   signiﬁcant for high-SNR detections at EDA (p = 0.17) or at EDA in 2022–2026 (p = 0.5). We
169   attribute it to an SNR-dependent bias of the peak-frequency estimate, but cannot exclude a real
170   modulation of ∼10–15 µHz (Supporting Information).


171   4     Discussion
172   4.1   How large should a tidal eﬀect be?
173   At EDA the modulation is about ±11% in power. Linear depth eﬀects of a 0.59 m M2 tide on
174   26 s swell are much smaller at shelf depths (Figure 4b). Primary-microseism forcing over a wavy
175   bottom changes by 0.011–0.112 dB at 20–50 m depth, 4–40 times less than observed; bottom
176   pressure and bed orbital velocity are modulated with the wrong sign (larger at low water); wave–
177   current interaction gives ≤0.07 dB at 20–50 m and peaks 3.1 h after high water. Reaching ∼0.45
178   dB with these linear mechanisms requires water only ∼3–8 m deep. Depth-limited (breaking-type)
179   forcing reaches 0.51 dB at 10 m mean depth, but applies only in the surf zone. This agrees with the
180   expectation of Poli et al. (2026, peer-review ﬁle) that depth changes of the order of a metre should
181   not aﬀect swell in tens of metres of water, and means that the tidal signal must arise either where
182   the water is very shallow (surf zone, estuaries, tidal ﬂats) or through a strongly nonlinear source.
183   For example, a source whose output depends exponentially on bottom pressure, ∝ exp(∆p/p0 ),
184   needs p0 ≈ 47–64 kPa for the observed 0.40–0.55 dB, given a tidal bottom-pressure amplitude of
185   5.9 kPa.

186   4.2   Constraints on the nonlinearity
187   The harmonic content limits the form of a nonlinear response. The observed M4/M2 ratio at
188   EDA (0.071) is close to that of the tide itself (0.054). One-sided thresholds or clipping of a tide-
189   driven forcing produce M4/M2 of 0.14–0.32 and are excluded, while a gently saturating symmetric
190   response (e.g. tanh(h/0.6 m) of tidal height h) gives γ = 0.70 with M4/M2 of 0.046 and is allowed.
191   Measuring in dB a response that is linear in power, or a response to tidal current rather than
192   height, gives γ ≥ 1 and cannot explain γ < 1; but, as noted, γ < 1 itself is not robust.

193   4.3   The lag
194   Maximum line power 1.6–1.8 h after M2 high water at Limbe is not explained by tidal height alone.
195   The tide in the Bight of Bonny is nearly in phase over the gauges we examined (within ±0.3 h),
196   so the lag must come from the source physics or from a local delay. A sum of a height-driven

                                                       5
197   term and an opposing-current term (peaking at +3.1 h for a standing tide) reproduces it if their
198   amplitudes are in the ratio 1.16–1.60. Tidal propagation into estuaries and creeks, which lags the
199   open coast, or a delayed (e.g. poroelastic) response are alternatives we cannot test without regional
200   tidal-current data.

201   4.4   Implications and tests
202   Any mechanism for the 26 s line must now explain a source-side modulation of about ±11% in
203   power that scales roughly with tidal range and lags high water by 1.6–1.8 h, together with the swell
204   control demonstrated by Poli et al. (2026). Swell interacting with very shallow coastal topography
205   would naturally provide both, but a nonlinear, swell-triggered resonator remains possible. Decisive
206   tests include (i) the phase relative to tidal currents from a validated regional tide model, (ii) the
207   modulation of swell-driven bursts as a function of incident swell height, and (iii) coastal or ocean-
208   bottom pressure records near the proposed source. As a side remark, both components of the
209   26 s doublet—whose splitting was already suggested by Holcomb (1998)—share the same tidal
210   modulation, and the dominant component switched from ∼26.3 s to ∼26.5 s in 2025 (Supporting
211   Information).

212   4.5   Limitations
213   The tide reference is a harmonic prediction from a single gauge record (2008–2011) rather than
214   contemporaneous observations. Modulation amplitudes at distant stations are small (0.1–0.3 dB)
215   and MBAR is marginal; station noise is correlated (hourly LMB correlation ∼0.5), so stations
216   are not independent replicates for second-order tests. The near-line background is itself weakly
217   modulated at EDA and TAM, and the scaling index γ depends on how background is removed.
218   The toy model is linear and deliberately simple.


219   5     Conclusions
220   The 26 s Gulf of Guinea microseism is modulated by the ocean tide. At the near-source station
221   EDA the line power varies by 0.47 dB (about ±11%) with a maximum 1.6 h after M2 high water,
222   a result that survives conservative block-resampling nulls, leave-one-year-out tests and changes of
223   metric. The same phase is found at four further stations up to 3670 km away, so the modulation is
224   a property of the source. Its amplitude grows with tidal range (γ ≈ 0.7–1; proportional scaling not
225   excluded). Linear depth eﬀects at shelf depths are 4–40 times too small, pointing to coupling in
226   very shallow water or to a nonlinear, mildly saturating source; threshold-like clipping is excluded
227   by the weak M4 response.
